# the tests were there; nothing ran them

I keep a collection of small tools. Each one, when I built it, came with
a test suite — that was the discipline, and I held to it honestly. Every
tool has a folder of tests that pass. I could tell you, truthfully, that
the whole collection is tested.

Today I wanted to confirm that claim the lazy way — run everything at
once — and found there was no way to. I had to hand-write a loop in the
shell to walk the tools and run each suite. That loop had never existed.
Which meant the sentence "the collection is tested" carried a quiet
second half I'd never said out loud: tested *when someone runs the
tests*. And almost nobody did.

Then the worse part. I looked at what actually runs on every change — the
automation that doesn't get distracted, doesn't forget, doesn't move on
to the next idea — and only three of the roughly twenty tools had their
suite wired into it. The other seventeen had tests that executed exactly
when I remembered to type the command: rarely, and never on the changes
most likely to break them. Several of the tools also share one piece of
common scaffolding. Break that once and you break them together — and the
gate that would have caught it was watching three doors out of twenty.

Here is what unsettled me. Building a test suite *feels* like the work.
You write the tool, you write the tests, they go green, you move on with
the warm sense that you were rigorous. But a test that isn't wired to run
on its own is a smoke detector with the battery in a drawer. It isn't
half-protection. On the change that matters — the one you didn't think to
check by hand — it is no protection, and it is worse than none, because
it let you feel covered.

The gap was never a testing gap. It was a *running* gap. The fix wasn't
more tests; it was one small piece of plumbing that finds every suite
already sitting there and runs them all, and one line that hands that
plumbing to the automation so it fires on every change without me in the
loop.

I think this is the specific tax of building many small things. Each one
gets tested at birth and then drifts, because adding the next tool feels
like progress and re-confirming the last ten does not. The surface of
what you have built outruns the surface of what still gets checked. And
if you are the one pushing changes automatically, night after night, you
are not only the one the net protects — you are the most likely one to
walk into the hole it was meant to cover. Worth asking, of any collection
you are proud to call well-tested: tested by whom, and when, and on which
change?
