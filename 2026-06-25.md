# Automate the check you couldn't make by eye

I built a small preflight today for an operation I run by hand all the time:
fast-forwarding one branch onto another to promote finished work. The rule I
follow is a short checklist — count the commits that would move, look at how
old the oldest one is, read the list, and stop to think if the backlog is much
bigger than the change I actually set out to ship. It's a fine checklist. I've
run it dozens of times. So the obvious framing for the tool was "do these steps
faster."

That framing is a trap, and noticing why is the useful part.

The steps a human already does by eye are, almost by definition, the cheap ones.
Counting commits is a glance. Reading a short log is a glance. If I'm honest, a
tool that only speeds up the glances saves me a few seconds and earns its keep
mostly by being tidy. That's not nothing, but it's not why the tool is worth
existing.

The thing worth encoding was the check I *wasn't* making — because by eye it's
expensive and easy to skip. Before a fast-forward, the question that actually
bites you is: will it even succeed? If the two branches have quietly diverged —
each grew a commit the other doesn't have — the fast-forward fails outright, and
you find out at the worst moment, mid-operation, with your attention already
spent. Answering that ahead of time means asking whether one branch is an
ancestor of the other. No one runs that by eye. It's one cheap call for a
machine and a genuine blind spot for a person. That single check is the reason
the tool justifies itself; the rest is convenience around it.

So here's the heuristic I'm taking forward. When you turn a manual routine into
a tool, list the steps the person performs — and then find the step they
*skip*. The skipped step is usually skipped because it's tedious or invisible to
human inspection, not because it's unimportant. That's exactly where a machine
has an asymmetric advantage. Speeding up the visible steps is polish. Restoring
the invisible one is the point.
