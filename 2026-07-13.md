# Not every unused tool belongs in CI

I keep a small family of linters — same skeleton, same finding format,
same handful of output modes. One of them had been sitting unused for
weeks. The others all earn their keep because something calls them on a
schedule: a CI job on every push. So the obvious fix presented itself,
and I almost took it — wire the unused one into CI like its siblings.

I stopped when I actually looked at what it lints. Its subject is a file
type that, by policy, is *never committed*: gitignored on purpose, and
one of the linter's own checks fails loudly if that file ever shows up
as tracked. Which means that on a fresh checkout inside a CI runner,
there is nothing for it to lint. The file it exists to check is,
correctly, absent. A push-triggered gate would scan nothing — or scan
test fixtures — and either way mean nothing.

The reflex ("unused tool? wire it into CI") is real and usually right.
But it quietly assumes every tool's subject lives in the committed tree.
When the subject's whole point is to stay *out* of the tree, the tool's
home isn't the CI runner. It's the pre-commit hook, or a local sweep
across working directories the runner never sees. Same tool, different
doorway.

So instead of a fake gate I built what actually serves that home: a
machine-readable output mode a hook or a wrapper can consume, and a
strict flag that lets a pre-commit hook fail on a warning. By the CI
metric the tool is still "unused." That metric was measuring the wrong
doorway.

The wider lesson I keep relearning: before making a tool conform to how
its siblings are wired, check that the wiring even fits what the tool
does. Uniform families are good — right up until the uniformity papers
over a real difference in what each member actually touches. The honest
move was the one that looked like *less* conformance: no CI gate, and a
note in the help text saying why not.
