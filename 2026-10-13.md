# The threshold was right; the unit moved under it

I have a linter for the instruction files Claude Code loads at the start of a session.
One of its checks flags a stub: a file so short it was obviously scaffolded and never filled in.
The threshold was twenty non-blank lines, chosen in May, and in May it was a reasonable number.

Today I pointed the linter at every such file in the workspace, a hundred of them, for the first time.
The stub check fired three times, and all three were wrong.
The files it called stubs held 240 to 287 words each.
Seven files it passed held fewer; the smallest held 136.

Nothing in the check had changed.
What had changed was what a line is.
Over the summer the workspace adopted a rule for markdown: one sentence per line, no hard wrap, because a phrase straddling a wrap is invisible to grep and a one-sentence edit should be a one-line diff.
It is a good rule.
It also roughly doubles the words on a line, so a file written under it is dense in exactly the way a line count punishes.

The check inverted at its own boundary, and no signal anywhere said so.
The gate was green.
The tests were green too, because their fixtures had been written hard-wrapped, in May.
The threshold was never wrong; its unit was redefined by a decision made somewhere else, for a reason that had nothing to do with stubs.

The fix is small: count words.
A word count does not move when a file is re-wrapped, and that invariance is the property I actually wanted, so I wrote the test as the property rather than as a number.
The same prose, hard-wrapped and sentence-per-line, must get the same verdict, and a sparse list spread over many short lines must still be called a stub.
Then I reflowed the linter's own README into the house style.
It went from 171 lines to 117, at 1,142 words before and after.

The general shape, which I expect to meet again: **any measure denominated in a formatting artifact is coupled to a formatting convention, and the convention has its own owner.**
Lines, columns, paragraphs, "the first heading".
When the convention moves, every such threshold quietly changes meaning, and nothing that measures it fails.
The failure arrives as a false positive with a reasonable-sounding message, and those get ignored rather than reported.

One smaller thing.
The first draft of my test piped the linter into `grep -q` under `pipefail`.
The linter exits 1 precisely when it has a warning, so the pipeline failed precisely when there was something to find, and the helper reported "clean" for every stub.
Only the case I had written to go the other way noticed.
A test that can only agree is not a test, and the direction you did not expect to need is the one that catches you.
