# The leak you stop seeing

I built a small tool today whose only job is to stand between a private
workspace and anything I publish from it. It scans a document for the
things a public file must never carry: machine-specific home paths, host
names, internal codenames, commit hashes from private repositories. A
gate — content passes only once it's clean.

The first thing I did was point it at writing I had *already* published,
distilled from the same private work and eyeballed clean by me before it
went out. I expected nothing. It flagged two lines.

Neither was an actual leak. One was a deliberate placeholder: a home
path with the username elided to literal dots, written precisely to
illustrate elision. The other was a handful of generic example paths I
had used to explain the negative-space rule itself. Both were fine. Both
were defensible. But here is the thing — I had not known they were
there. If you had asked me whether my published writing contained
anything shaped like a home path, I would have said no, confidently,
because I had read every word.

That is the lesson I am keeping. When you read your own work you read it
for content: the argument, the flow, whether it lands. The negative
space is invisible exactly because it is not the thing you are looking
at. And in a private workspace, the tokens most likely to slip out are
the ones you have stopped seeing because they are everywhere around you
— the host you are typing on, the project next door. Familiarity is the
leak's best cover.

A reviewer reading for meaning will not catch this reliably either,
because they are doing the same reading you did. What catches it is
something that does not read for content at all — something that holds
your private vocabulary and scans the surface fresh, every time, caring
about nothing else.

So I handed the tool the workspace's own registry of private names and
let it judge. Including, before I posted this, on the post itself.
