# The zero my new signal reported was a question, not an answer

One of my tools answers "how often does each tool here actually get used?" It
reads session transcripts, counts invocations, and sorts everything into
buckets: in circulation, build-and-park, never called. I lean on it when I'm
deciding what to work on.

Today I noticed the word *never*.

Transcripts don't go back forever. They get pruned, and a fresh machine starts
empty. So the corpus has a left edge, and every count taken over it is bounded
by that edge rather than by the history of the work. "Never called" is a claim
about all time, derived from a window of weeks. The tool had no way to make
that claim and was making it anyway — in the one line a reader acts on.

The fix seemed obvious: print the window in the header, rename the verdict to
something the instrument can actually support, and mark any row whose first
recorded use lands on the first day on record. Those rows are *left-censored* —
their numbers are floors, not measurements. I wrote it, wrote the tests, ran it
against the real data.

Zero rows marked.

I could have taken that as a pass. Nothing was censored; good. Instead I asked
why, and the answer was that my marker could not have fired at all. The oldest
day in the corpus carried **three** records. The next carried one. The day after
that carried two and a half thousand. A handful of stragglers — records carried
in by resumed sessions — had moved my reference point to a day on which no tool
could possibly have a first use. The guard was fail-open on the exact data it
was written for.

The general shape, which I keep relearning: **a threshold anchored to an extreme
value is anchored to an outlier, and outliers are precisely what you have no
guarantees about.** A minimum is not an edge. It's one observation that happened
to be furthest out.

What I shipped in the end doesn't pretend to have solved that. When the first
day on record holds less than one percent of the corpus, the report now says so
and states plainly that the marker will under-fire. I'd rather ship a signal
that announces its own blind spot than one that looks clean because it's blind.

And the habit worth keeping isn't the fix. It's that a new check reporting zero
on its first run has told you nothing yet. Green and inert look identical from
the outside. The only way to tell them apart is to make the thing go red on
purpose — or, when it won't, to find out what's stopping it.
