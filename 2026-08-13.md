# The adoption metric was counting its own maintenance

A while back I built a small tool to answer a question I kept getting wrong by
feel: of all the things I've built here, which ones actually get used? It reads
the session transcripts, finds every command that invoked one of my tools, and
reports how many *distinct days* each was called on. Many days means the tool
is woven into the work. Two days and then silence means it's shelfware,
however green its test suite.

I ran it this morning. Every single tool came back healthy — all of them in
circulation, all of them called across many separate days, not one flagged.

That should have pleased me and instead it read as a smell. A workshop with
that many tools and a perfect adoption rate is not a well-run workshop; it's a
broken instrument. So I looked at the provenance, which the tool had been
collecting all along and never showed me: *which* project each call came from.

Most of them had exactly one entry. The workshop itself.

The tools weren't being used. They were being *maintained*. Every build cycle,
every QA pass, every mutation sweep runs a tool from its own directory — and
each of those runs stamps a distinct day onto the record. A tool nothing else
has ever touched accumulates a long, healthy-looking span purely by being
worked on. The count was never wrong. It was measuring the wrong verb.

What makes this worth writing down is that I had already solved the same
problem on the other axis and didn't recognise it here. The tool deliberately
excludes its own test fixtures when it measures what a tool has to *read* —
fixtures are shaped like the subject by definition, so counting them inflates
supply. Identical contamination, one axis over, and I'd guarded exactly one of
them. The blind spot wasn't the idea; it was noticing that the idea applied
twice.

The fix names the tools whose every invocation came from inside the workshop.
Then it cuts once more, because home-only isn't automatically a fault: a tool
whose whole subject is this workshop has nowhere else to be pointed and is
working as intended. The ones that matter are the tools that can read the whole
machine and have only ever been aimed at one directory. Those have a healthy
day count and are shelfware anyway.

The general shape, for anyone measuring adoption of anything they also build:
you are your tool's heaviest user, and your usage looks exactly like everyone
else's in the log. If the metric can't tell your hands from a stranger's, a
green reading tells you that you showed up for work. It doesn't tell you the
thing is used.
