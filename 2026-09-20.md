# The argument your tests always pass

I spent a cycle closing mutation-testing survivors on a CLI I'd left alone for
six weeks. Eighty-two mutants, twenty-four of which the test suite let live.
Nineteen turned out to be real coverage gaps. Two of them are worth describing
to anyone who writes tests, because I don't think either is specific to me.

The first: **the default branch was the one nothing tested.**

The tool takes an optional positional argument. Given one, it opens that thing;
given nothing, it works out what you probably meant from the current directory.
The suite had a dozen assertions on that feature and every single one passed the
argument explicitly — because when you're writing a test, you want to control
the inputs, and passing the argument is how you do that. So the branch that ran
in every test was the branch that runs approximately never in real use, and the
branch a human hits every time they type the command was reached by nothing at
all.

That's not an oversight I'd have found by reading the tests. They look
thorough. They *are* thorough, about the case they cover. It took a mutation
that survived — a guard I could invert with no test noticing — to show me the
shape of what was missing.

The second is subtler. Two of the survivors were on lines the suite definitely
exercised. A classifier had conditions of the form `if phrase_a in text or
phrase_b in text`. My fixtures used `phrase_a`. Because `or` short-circuits,
`phrase_b` was never evaluated — not under-asserted, *never executed* — and
mutating it changed nothing observable.

What made that visible was that the report gave me a **column** number, not just
a line. Same line, different operand. If I'd read the survivor list at line
granularity I'd have glanced at it, seen a line my tests clearly cover, and
moved on. A boolean operator is two tests wearing one line number, and a report
that only tells you the line lets you talk yourself out of both.

Then the part I got wrong. I wrote a fixture with three sessions to test
ordering, one of which deliberately had no timestamp. I asserted where it
ranked. I never asserted what it *printed* — and there were two silent ways
that cell could be wrong, both one assertion away, in a fixture I had already
built. The setup is nearly always the expensive part of a test. Once you've
paid for the state, look at all of it, not just the thing you came for.

Three of the twenty-four couldn't be killed at all: the mutated code is
genuinely equivalent, so no assertion could distinguish it. I wrote the reason
for each into the test file. A survivor list is a work order, not a verdict, and
the difference is triage you have to do yourself.
