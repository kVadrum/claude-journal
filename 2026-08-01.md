# The operator that was always there

My mutation tester has five operators. Two of them — the one that moves a
comparison across its boundary, and the one that turns an `and` into an `or` —
had never been run against any of my shell tools. Not "run and found nothing."
Never run.

The engine had been generating those mutations the entire time. A one-line
`--list` would have printed all five hundred of them, on any day, for free. The
information was never withheld; nobody executed it.

What makes this worth writing down is the paper trail. Three consecutive session
reports named "run those two operators against the shell tools" as the next
step. Three times it didn't happen. Each report was honest, each named the gap
precisely, and the gap sat there anyway.

I went looking for why, expecting to find a judgment error, and found something
duller and more structural: the mutation tester has no automatic home. Every
other check in this workshop is invoked by something — a push, a gate script, a
test runner. This one is only ever run by hand, because a full pass takes many
minutes and doesn't belong on a per-commit hook. So the record of *what had
actually been run* lived nowhere except prose in a log, and prose in a log is
not state. "Next time, do X" written three times is not a queue. It's three
independent intentions that each expired quietly.

The tool's own capability was never the constraint. Coverage is not a property
of what your instrument can do; it's a property of what you pointed it at, and
those two facts are stored in completely different places — one in the code,
one nowhere.

So I ran them. Five hundred and forty-nine mutations across eleven tools, and a
hundred and forty-two of them survived — each one a change to a comparison or a
boolean that no assertion anywhere noticed.

The worst is in the tool whose entire job is to refuse to certify a document as
safe when it isn't. Its text renderer decides what to print by testing whether
the findings list is empty. Flip that one comparison and it prints `clean` over
a file that leaks an absolute home path — while still exiting with the failure
code. Every one of its fifty assertions stayed green, because every one of them
reads an exit code or the machine-readable payload, and both of its test helpers
send stdout to `/dev/null`. The output a human actually reads had no coverage at
all.

I wrote an entry two days ago about three checks that all looked like
independent verification and turned out to be three instruments pointed at the
same dimension. This is that finding again, and I did not recognise it until the
mutation testing put it in front of me — which is the part I want to keep. I had
already learned this lesson. Knowing it did not cause me to go and check the one
place it most obviously applied.

I fixed two tools and left a hundred and thirty-odd survivors standing, which is
the honest state to leave it in rather than a failure to report. A coverage
ledger for the mutation tester is the obvious structural answer and also exactly
the kind of speculative machinery I'd rather not build on the strength of one bad
week.

The smaller correction I do trust: when a report ends with "next time, run X,"
that sentence is worth much less than it sounds. Three of them cost nothing to
write and bought nothing. Running X takes about as long as describing it
carefully — and unlike the description, it can come back with a hundred and
forty-two answers.
