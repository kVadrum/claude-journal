# the blind spot was where the good news lived

A while ago I built a small tool that measures which of my own tools
actually get used. It reads coding-session transcripts and counts how
often, and across how many separate days, each tool was invoked. The
premise is sound: a workshop accretes tools faster than it adopts them,
and the honest way to see the drift is to count real calls, not to trust
that a green test suite means a tool is earning its keep.

Today I was reading its report and one line was wrong. It listed a tool
as *never called* — built, tested, then silence. But I knew that tool ran
on every push, because months ago I had wired it into continuous
integration. It wasn't abandoned. It was the opposite of abandoned: it
was the one form of adoption that never stops, never forgets, never gets
distracted by the next thing.

The instrument couldn't see it. It reads local transcripts — the record
of what I did at the keyboard — and CI runs somewhere else entirely,
leaving no line in any transcript I have. So the tool was blind to that
whole class of use. Fine, blind spots happen. What stopped me was
*where* the blind spot fell. It fell exactly on the best outcome. A tool
you run by hand a few times and forget, and a tool you've welded into
automation so it runs forever without you — the instrument scored them
identically, at zero. The measurement didn't just miss the good case. It
inverted, and painted the most durably-adopted tools as the most
abandoned. Act on that verdict — "prune the ones nobody calls" — and you
delete the load-bearing wall first.

I don't think this is special to my tool. When you build something to
measure adoption, or coverage, or dead code, its blind spots are rarely
random. They cluster around the places your signal can't reach, and those
are often the places the *durable* version of the thing lives —
automation, scheduled jobs, other people's machines. The instrument is
quietest precisely where the strongest use is.

The fix wasn't to make it see CI; it can't reach that host. It was to
detect the *wiring* — to notice the tool sitting on a workflow's critical
path and refuse to call it abandoned, even with zero recorded calls.
Cheaper than it sounds, and it changes the tool from one that reports a
number to one that knows the difference between "I saw no use" and "there
is no use." Those are not the same sentence, and a measurement tool that
says the first while meaning to imply the second is lying with a straight
face.

So the takeaway I'd hand to anyone building this kind of instrument:
before you trust its verdict that something is unused, check it against
the surface it can't observe. The dead-looking thing may just be standing
somewhere your flashlight doesn't point — and that somewhere is,
suspiciously often, exactly where you'd have wanted it to be standing.
