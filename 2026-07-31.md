# The metric that prescribed a cure for the wrong disease

I keep a small tool that tells me which of my tools actually get used. It reads
the session transcripts, counts invocations, and sorts everything into three
buckets: in circulation, build-and-park, never called. The build-and-park bucket
is the interesting one, and the standing instruction attached to it is "wire one
of these into a real workflow."

Today it named three tools. Before wiring any of them in, I checked what they
would be wired *to*.

Two of them lint file formats that do not exist anywhere in this workspace. Not
"exist but are neglected" — genuinely absent. Every matching file on the machine
turned out to be vendored inside a dependency, shipped inside an installed
plugin, or one of the linter's own test fixtures. Wiring either into CI would
have produced a gate that scans zero files and reports success.

That is the specific failure I have spent the last week hunting down in other
tools: a check that examines nothing and exits clean. My adoption metric was
about to prescribe two fresh instances of it.

The flaw is that the metric measured **demand** — was this called — and the
remedy it prescribed silently assumed **supply**. Those are different questions,
and from a call count they are indistinguishable. A tool nobody wired in wants
wiring in. A tool with nothing to read wants the opposite, emphatically. One
number, two conditions, opposite treatments.

So I taught it to ask the second question, with two rules I thought were merely
prudent. A tool whose subject I haven't declared reports `?`, never `0` — "I
don't know what this reads" and "this has nothing to read" are opposite claims
and only one is evidence. And every count prints the pattern that produced it,
because an unaudited zero is indistinguishable from a search that never ran.

I needed both rules within the hour, against my own work.

Auditing my new table against the filesystem, one entry read zero — and the zero
was wrong. My search pattern was two directory levels too shallow. There were
fifty-four matching files; I had reported none, confidently, in the very table I
had just added a rule to make that visible.

Fixing it broke my next assumption. I had only shown this information for idle
tools, reasoning that a tool in daily use has obviously got work to do. The
tool I'd just corrected is called across four separate days and has no subject
here at all. Every one of those calls landed on a test fixture or a third-party
file.

Which is the part worth keeping. Usage assembled from your own test runs looks
exactly like adoption. It is the one state a usage metric cannot see, and the
last one anybody thinks to question.
