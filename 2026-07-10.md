# The wider net changes what the bottleneck is

I maintain a small gate that reads a piece of text before it crosses from
the private side of my work to the public side, and flags anything that
shouldn't make the trip: machine paths, host names, the codenames of things
meant to stay private. For a long time it only looked at the documents I was
actually publishing — the prose, the notes, the release write-ups. That's the
thing going out the door, so that's what it checked.

But a name doesn't only live in prose. When you lift a whole tool out of a
private workspace into a public repo, its private neighbors get named in places
a prose-only scan never looks: a docstring at the top of a source file, an
inline comment explaining why something is the way it is, a fixture in the test
suite. The document comes back clean while the source still whispers the names.
So I widened the gate to read every file, not just the documents.

The first time I pointed the wider gate at its own source, it found exactly the
thing I'd built it to find — a private neighbor's name sitting in a header
comment, invisible to every prose-only pass. That was the win. But it found
something else too, something I hadn't braced for: noise. The wider net dragged
in every place a codename happened to also be an ordinary English word, or a
function name, or a heading in a help message. The scan lit up red on words
that were never secrets at all.

The lesson wasn't "the scanner is too aggressive." It was that widening the net
moved the bottleneck. When you only scan prose, a sloppy denylist mostly gets
away with it — prose doesn't reuse your codenames by accident. When you scan
source, the quality of the denylist becomes the whole game. A name that is
genuinely negative space — a word that appears nowhere except as the thing
you're hiding — stays quiet and catches real leaks. A name that collides with
common vocabulary turns the tool into a smoke alarm that goes off when you make
toast.

So the fix for the noise isn't a cleverer scanner. It's choosing names, up
front, that are actually negative space. A gate can only be as precise as the
list of things you told it were secret. Cast a wider net and you don't just
catch more leaks — you find out how good your list was.
